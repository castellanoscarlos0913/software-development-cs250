Sprint Review & Retrospective
ChadaTech has historically utilized a traditional Waterfall development model to deliver custom software solutions for domestic and international clients. While this approach provided rigid structure and predictable milestone scheduling, it created significant operational friction as market demands evolved. To build a more cohesive, adaptable corporate culture, ChadaTech initiated a pilot program to transition from Waterfall to the Scrum-Agile framework, selecting my development team to lead this pilot during the development of a web application for SNHU Travel, a travel agency seeking innovative digital tools to expand its client base. As the Scrum Master leading this project, I facilitated the iterative execution of the SNHU Travel application while navigating the cultural and operational shifts inherent in moving away from Waterfall execution.
Transitioning ChadaTech to an Agile environment requires establishing clear boundaries and distinct responsibilities across key Scrum roles. In traditional Waterfall projects, project managers often blended scope ownership, timeline enforcement, and technical direction into a single role, leading to misaligned expectations and slow feedback loops. Under Scrum, the Product Owner acted as the sole voice of the customer, translating client feature requests into discrete, prioritized user stories and protecting the development team from conflicting stakeholder demands. Serving as a servant-leader, my role as Scrum Master was to facilitate daily Scrum events, remove technical impediments, and protect the team’s sprint commitments from external scope creep, such as facilitating technical troubleshooting sessions when team members faced environment configuration errors. Meanwhile, the cross-functional Development and Testing Team held collective ownership over building and verifying working software increments, collaborating early in each sprint so that QA testers defined acceptance criteria alongside developers before code execution.
The adoption of a Scrum-Agile approach transformed how user stories progressed through the Software Development Life Cycle. Under Waterfall, the life cycle proceeded in rigid, sequential phases where software was not tested or executable until late in the project timeline, whereas Scrum compressed the entire process into repeating two-week sprint cycles. For the SNHU Travel application, user stories were defined according to explicit acceptance criteria and evaluated against a strict Definition of Done. For instance, during the development of the Top 5 Destinations Slide Show component, the team refined and estimated the user story during Sprint Planning, built the Java Swing UI components while validating image asset handling, and verified the feature within the sprint. The story met the Definition of Done when the slideshow executed seamlessly without missing image exceptions, source code comments were validated, and the output executable passed peer review, providing immediate visual proof of progress to SNHU Travel stakeholders.
One of the greatest limitations of ChadaTech’s prior Waterfall methodology was its inability to accommodate changing requirements mid-project without costly change requests and schedule slippage. Under Scrum-Agile, mid-sprint interruptions and shifting customer priorities were managed effectively without compromising project momentum. During the development of the SNHU Travel application, when client feedback required updating display layouts and modifying resource file handling for destination image packages, the team managed the pivot through structured Agile channels. Active work in the Sprint Backlog was protected from immediate mid-sprint scope changes by deferring emergency feature tweaks to the Product Owner for future sprint refinement. Additionally, when developers encountered execution errors relating to missing image files or environment export paths, the impediment was raised immediately during the Daily Stand-up, allowing the team to adjust daily task assignments, implement standardized relative file paths, and bundle resources cleanly into application archives during the current sprint.
Open, continuous communication served as the foundation of our Scrum team’s success. Moving away from formal, infrequent status reports, the team utilized daily, lightweight communication channels that promoted radical transparency and psychological safety. The Daily Stand-up kept developers and testers synchronized on daily commitments and blockers, preventing team members from working in isolated specialist silos. By integrating QA testers directly into user story discussions from the start, cross-functional feedback loops were significantly shortened, allowing testers to provide edge-case scenarios directly to developers before coding began. Furthermore, at the close of each sprint, the team conducted open Sprint Retrospectives to discuss process friction points, resulting in actionable improvements such as establishing shared coding standards and standardizing project archive structures.
To support project coordination, ChadaTech leveraged digital project management platforms and information radiators, such as digital Kanban boards, which served as a central source of truth. Tracking items across To Do, In Progress, In Review, and Done swimlanes ensured that any team member or stakeholder could immediately assess sprint status without requesting manual status updates. Furthermore, Agile tracking tools automatically generated burndown charts and velocity metrics, ensuring that future sprint planning was driven by historical team velocity rather than unrealistic management estimates. These organizational tools reinforced key tenets of the Agile Manifesto, specifically prioritizing working software over comprehensive documentation and responding to change over following a plan.
Analyzing the outcomes of the SNHU Travel pilot project reveals clear advantages as well as specific operational challenges associated with the Scrum-Agile framework. On the positive side, early and continuous delivery of working application increments allowed stakeholders to test real software every two weeks, defects and environment compatibility errors were exposed and resolved early within short sprint cycles, and individual knowledge silos were dismantled in favor of shared accountability. On the other hand, the framework required continuous active involvement from client stakeholders and the Product Owner, risked feature creep if the Product Owner did not maintain strict backlog filtering, and presented an initial learning curve as team members adjusted to daily stand-ups and refinement sessions.
Ultimately, the Scrum-Agile framework was the best approach for the SNHU Travel development project, providing the structure necessary to maintain steady velocity while absorbing changes gracefully. Based on the empirical success of this pilot project, it is recommended that ChadaTech expand the Scrum-Agile framework across all corporate development teams. By investing in formal Agile role training and adopting standardized project management software, ChadaTech can eliminate the illusion of progress common to its legacy Waterfall projects, improve software release quality, and foster a transparent, highly responsive engineering culture.
